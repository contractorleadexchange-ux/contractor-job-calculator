Contractor markup vs. margin: how much should you actually quote?

Markup compares gross profit with job cost. Gross margin compares gross profit with your pre-tax selling price. Use the two calculators below, then work through a real-world-style bid example to avoid underpricing your next job.

Markup → margin

Target margin → required selling price

1. The markup and margin formulas

Markup (%) = (pre-tax selling price − direct job cost) ÷ direct job cost × 100.

Gross margin (%) = (pre-tax selling price − direct job cost) ÷ pre-tax selling price × 100.

Selling price for a target margin = direct job cost ÷ (1 − target margin as a decimal).

A target 30% gross margin means dividing direct cost by 0.70; it does not mean adding a 30% markup. To calculate a target gross margin, the percentage must be less than 100%. These calculations use the selling price before sales tax collected from the customer.

2. Worked bid: a $15,000 job-cost mistake

A hypothetical contractor estimates $15,000 of direct costs (materials, loaded field labor, subcontractors, and job-specific expenses) and wants a 30% gross margin. See how mistakenly applying 30% markup changes the bid:

3. Stress test: what if direct job costs rise 10%?

Suppose the contractor submits the $21,428.57 fixed-price quote, but field labor or material waste raises direct costs by 10%, from $15,000 to $16,500. With no approved change order, the price stays fixed.

A correct pricing formula cannot compensate for underestimated crew hours, material waste, scope omissions, or unmanaged change orders. Build realistic allowances into the estimate before deciding whether a margin is sufficient.

4. What belongs in direct job cost?

Include costs attributable to delivering the project: materials, subcontractors, job-specific permits, rented equipment, and employer-loaded field labor rather than wage rates alone. Be consistent about which costs you include, and never double-count labor burden already allocated to the job.

Do not add customer sales tax to the selling-price denominator when measuring gross margin. Gross margin is not net profit: office payroll, general insurance, vehicles, marketing, software, B&O tax, callbacks, financing costs, and other overhead still need coverage.

Three checks before submitting a bid

  1. Estimate materials, field labor burden and job-specific costs completely.
  2. Choose whether the pricing target is markup or margin; do not interchange them.
  3. Run at least one realistic labor or materials overrun before finalizing scope, terms, and price.

Frequently asked questions

Does 30% markup mean a 30% gross margin?

No. On $15,000 direct cost, a 30% markup gives a $19,500 selling price and roughly a 23.1% gross margin.

Should sales tax be included when calculating margin?

No. In these planning examples, use the pre-tax contract price; sales tax separately collected for remittance is not earned job revenue.

Can the formula tell me the best price to charge?

No. It computes a price for your own cost and target assumptions. Market demand, project risk, competitive options, cash flow, warranties, overhead and customer value still matter.

What if the required selling price looks too high?

Recheck scope, quantities, job costs and the target margin. A mathematically correct price is not a guarantee of market acceptance; don't lower your target without understanding what the change costs your business.
